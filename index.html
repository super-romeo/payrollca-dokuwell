How to do payroll in Canada for a small business

A small employer in Canada usually handles payroll in one of three ways: a spreadsheet, the owner's accountant, or payroll software. With a spreadsheet, every pay run means calculating deductions by hand and remembering when money is due to the Canada Revenue Agency. PayRun Canada is built for owners who want each pay run to take minutes, not an evening.

Doing payroll in Canada comes down to the same steps every pay period: work out gross pay, deduct Canada Pension Plan contributions, Employment Insurance premiums and income tax, pay the employee, and remit the deductions together with the employer's share to the Canada Revenue Agency. Most small employers remit by the 15th of the month after the month they paid their employees. The dates for remittances, T4 slips and Records of Employment are in the questions below, and you can start with your first employee.

You add your employees once: pay type, pay rate, province and pay schedule — weekly, every two weeks, twice a month or monthly. For each pay run you enter hours or confirm the salary, add overtime, vacation pay or a bonus if there is one, and the service calculates gross pay, deductions and net pay. Each employee receives a pay stub by email.

After every pay run you see what has to be remitted to the CRA and by when. A reminder comes before the due date, so the remittance is not forgotten in a busy week. All pay runs are stored, so you can always see what was paid to whom and what was remitted.

At year end the T4 and T4A slips are prepared from the year's pay runs. You review them, correct anything that changed during the year, and file them; employees get their copies. When an employee leaves, the Record of Employment is prepared from the pay history.

The service keeps employee records in one place: start date, pay rate changes, vacation accrued and taken. When the accountant asks for payroll figures for the year, you export them instead of rebuilding them from bank statements.

PayRun Canada is meant for businesses with one to about twenty employees: cafés, shops, trades, clinics and small offices. If you already work with an accountant, they can be invited to review pay runs and year-end slips.

Pricing is per month with a small amount per employee, so a business with two employees does not pay the same as a business with twenty. You can stop at any time; your pay history stays available for export.

Getting started takes one sitting. You enter your business details and each employee once, and if you are switching in the middle of the year you add the amounts already paid this year, so the year-end slips include the whole year and not only the months since you started. A first trial pay run shows the figures before anything is approved, and you can compare it with your last pay stubs to see that everything lines up.

Questions

How do I do payroll in Canada for a small business?

For each pay period you calculate gross pay, deduct CPP contributions, EI premiums and income tax, pay the net amount, and remit the deductions with the employer's share of CPP and EI to the Canada Revenue Agency. The service does these calculations for every pay run.

When are payroll remittances due to the CRA?

For most small employers (regular remitters) the deductions are due by the 15th of the month after the month in which employees were paid. If the 15th falls on a weekend or holiday, the due date is the next business day.

When are T4 slips due?

T4 slips and the T4 summary are due by the last day of February after the tax year. The service prepares them from the year's pay runs for you to review and file.

When do I have to issue a Record of Employment?

When an employee has an interruption of earnings, such as a layoff, a resignation or a leave. An electronic ROE is due within five calendar days after the end of the pay period in which the interruption happens; the service prepares it from the pay history.

Can my accountant see the pay runs?

Yes, you can invite your accountant to review pay runs and year-end slips.
